# When a large language model encounters wireless signal recognition: how can an LLM not touch the signal data command AI to adaptive modulation recognition? [with python code]

1. Introduction: Why is online modulation recognition so challenging? Automatic Modulation Recognition (AMR) is a key technology in wireless communication reception. Without knowing the modulation method used by the transmitter, the receiver must automatically infer the modulation type from the raw IQ signal, which is fundamental for applications such as spectrum monitoring, electronic reconnaissance, and cognitive radio.
In recent years, deep learning-based AMR methods have made significant progress. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have achieved recognition accuracy on standard datasets that surpasses traditional feature engineering methods. However, these methods typically assume an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) relationship between training data and deployment environment—that is, the signal distribution encountered during testing is exactly the same as during training.
Real-world wireless communication environments are far from ideal. In practical deployment, the receiver faces three challenges:
1. First, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) dynamically changes. The signal undergoes significant fluctuations in SNR due to factors such as distance changes, moving obstacles, and multipath effects during transmission. An identificator trained at a high SNR of 18dB may completely fail at a low SNR of -5dB.
2. Second, channel conditions continuously drift. In addition to SNR changes, gain decay, phase rotation, carrier frequency offset (CFO), and other channel parameters also change slowly or suddenly over time, causing the statistical characteristics of the signal at the receiving end to gradually deviate from the training distribution. This "concept drift" is one of the most challenging issues in the field of continuous learning.
3. Third, unknown modulation types may emerge. In scenarios such as spectrum monitoring, new modulation methods not included in the training set may appear at any time. Traditional classifiers can only make judgments within their closed category set, inevitably making mistakes when faced with new categories in an open environment.
These challenges combined make online adaptive modulation recognition a highly difficult problem. The system needs to establish recognition capabilities using limited labeled samples at the initial stage and must also have the ability to actively select the most valuable samples for labeling from streaming data after deployment, which is called online active learning. The core difficulty of active learning lies in: how to determine which unlabeled samples are most valuable for improving the current model?
II. Core Innovation: The core idea of the IQ-Free control plane Agentic AMR framework can be summarized in one sentence: separating signal recognition and policy control thoroughly.
Traditional end-to-end methods attempt to use a unified model to complete both recognition tasks and decision-making tasks simultaneously. The problem with this approach is that it's extremely difficult for a single model to understand the low-level physical features of raw IQ signals and perform high-level policy reasoning at the same time. Agentic AMR proposes a hierarchical separation architecture, separating the system into two independent operating planes:
Signal Identification Plane (Data Plane): Responsible for direct processing of raw IQ signals, the small CNN classifier outputs the predicted probability of modulation category and feature vector. This CNN does one thing: signal classification.
Strategy Control Plane (Control Plane): The "commander" is a frozen large language model (LLM). It does not directly interact with raw IQ signals, nor does it know what the signals look like. Instead, it reads compact text summaries constructed from the statistical quantities output by the CNN—we refer to them as "RF State Cards." Then, it analyzes the current health status of the system and plans which diagnostic tools should be called upon.
The beauty of this architecture lies in its clear delineation of responsibility boundaries: the LLM takes charge of "overall perspective"—what issues are currently facing the system? Which samples or regions in the feature space should be prioritized over others? Meanwhile, the CNN handles "micro-execution"—specific signal classification. The LLM never outputs modulation labels, and the CNN also does not need to understand global strategies. Both communicate information through a unified text interface, achieving complete decoupling at the cognitive level.
Method Details: RF State Card and Tool Planning 3.1 RF State Card: Translating the physical signal world into language
The controller cannot directly read IQ signals; how then does it perceive the current wireless environment? The answer lies in the RF State Card and Candidate Card, a set of carefully designed abstractions.
RF status card contains the following key information: current time step and total steps (to inform the LLM of which stage in the process it is currently in); estimation of signal-to-noise ratio and its standard deviation (reflecting the overall level and fluctuation of signal quality); channel drift level (a scalar from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates stable channels and 1 indicates maximum drift); classification error calibration (ECE, Expected Calibration Error, measuring whether the confidence of classification output matches its true accuracy); number of labeled samples for each class; and remaining label budget. These pieces of information are presented in a straightforward numerical and textual format that the LLM can fully understand within a single token window.
Candidate pool cards describe the statistical characteristics of a batch of unlabeled samples: the total number of samples in the pool, mean and standard deviation of prediction entropy (reflecting uncertainty level), mean of marginal uncertainty (measuring the degree of hesitation between different classes), feature space coverage gap (measuring how many unlabeled samples fall outside the distribution of labeled samples), and imbalanced labeling information (which modulation types have significantly insufficient labeled samples).
The design of this set of status cards follows a key principle: information is sufficient but concise. Each numerical value directly corresponds to an operable diagnostic dimension, eliminating the need for the LLM to "discover" patterns from massive raw data, providing a precise, condensed summary of system conditions.
3.2 Toolset: Five major criteria for LLM controller planning are not arbitrary abstract actions, but rather a combination of weighted sets of radio frequency tools. The system defines five fundamental criteria for acquisition:
Entropy criterion (Entropy): Measuring the uncertainty of the classification model's prediction probability distribution for individual samples. The higher the entropy value, the less certain the classification model is about a sample, and labeling such samples can reduce model uncertainty to the greatest extent.
Margin criterion (Margin): Calculating the difference between the most likely two categories predicted by the classification model. The smaller the margin, the more additional information is needed to confirm the classification boundary.
Coreset: Calculates the minimum distance between samples in the feature space and labeled samples. The farther the distance, the more samples are in a "new region" of the feature space, indicating that labeling them can effectively expand the model's coverage range.
Class Balance: Prioritizes samples with the highest predicted probability in the labeled set, which have fewer samples, to alleviate class imbalance caused by model bias.
Random: Randomly selects as a reference baseline.
All criteria scores are normalized to the [0,1] interval. The task of the LLM controller is to output soft weight vectors (e.g., [0.5, 0.1, 0.3, 0.1, 0]) based on the RF status card reflecting system conditions, then generate a comprehensive collection score by mixing these weights according to deterministic selection module, selecting top-k samples for manual annotation.
Decision logic of LLM controller: The LLM controller internally encodes rich radio frequency diagnostic strategies. When it reads an ECE value exceeding 0.15 (indicating severe miscalibration of the classifier) from the RF status card, it reduces the weights of uncertainty metrics such as entropy and marginals - because the high confidence of the classifier at this time is unreliable - and instead increases the weights of coreset and class balance, among other criteria dependent on feature space structure. When detecting high channel drift levels, it increases the weight of coreset criteria to encourage the system to actively explore new regions in the feature space to cope with distribution changes. When finding that a certain category has far fewer samples than others, it increases the weight of class balance criteria to compensate for the imbalance in labeling.
This strategy decision is entirely based on auditable text reasoning: Each step of the LLM's decision has a clear text explanation, and corresponding numerical weights can be traced back to specific state indicators directly. This explainability is particularly important in safety-critical radio frequency applications.
4. Environmental Design: Non-Stationary Drift Simulation
To fully assess the system's adaptability, Agentic AMR has defined a comprehensive set of non-stationary drift scenarios generators, covering five typical drift modes:  
SNR Ramp: Simulates the triangular SNR scan caused by the periodic change in the distance between the signal source and the receiver, with the SNR gradually increasing from 0dB to 18dB before dropping back to 0dB, forming a complete "tidal" cycle.  
SNR Step: Simulates the sudden entry of the receiver into a radio silence or strong interference environment, causing an abrupt change in the SNR at a certain moment.  
Channel Drift: Simulates the gradual degradation of channel parameters, including the stepwise deterioration of gain reduction, cumulative offset of phase rotation, slow drift of carrier frequency, and increase in additive noise power. These four channel effects vary independently and may simultaneously affect the signal.  
Class Emergence: Simulates the emergence of high-level modulation (such as 64QAM, 256QAM) during spectrum monitoring, testing the system's ability to adapt to new categories in an open environment.  
Mixed: The combination of these various drift modes simulates the most complex real-world scenario.  
Each drift mode is repeated on multiple random seeds (using 5 random seeds for each experiment), ensuring the statistical reliability of the results. Additionally, the system has been designed with strict label isolation mechanisms: samples that have been queried and labeled will not reappear in the candidate pool, and calibration set samples will never be used for model training to avoid information leakage.
5. Experimental Results and Performance Analysis
5.1 Intention Recognition Benchmark Testing
Agentic AMR was first evaluated on the intention recognition benchmark (Intent Benchmark). This benchmark consists of 78 scenarios (38 basic scenarios and 40 novel scenarios), each described in natural language with the current RF state and system requirements, for which the LLM controller needs to output the correct tool combination. The evaluation metric is the F1 score of the tool set - that is, the harmonic mean of precision and recall between the predicted tool combination and the labeled combination.
The experimental results were remarkable. Five LLM controllers with parameter sizes ranging from 7B to 30B achieved a tool set F1 score of 0.921 on novel scenarios, significantly surpassing all neural network-trained baseline routers. The best performing GPT-5.5 reached an F1 score of 0.921, while the weakest Mistral-7B also achieved 0.856. In contrast, the optimal neural network router (a multi-label classifier based on character-level features) only achieved a novel F1 score of 0.567, while the neural network router trained on textual statistical features (TF-IDF) also only achieved a composite training score of 0.649.
This gap reveals the core advantage of LLM over traditional supervised models: zero-shot combination generalization ability. Neural network routers can only make inference based on the scene patterns seen during training, and their performance drops sharply when encountering tool combinations not covered by the training set. In contrast, LLM controllers, with their semantic understanding of physical concepts (e.g., signal-to-noise ratio, calibration, drift, diversity, etc.), can infer reasonable tool combination strategies without any additional training.
5.2 Blind Re-labeling Validation
To eliminate the possibility that labeling biases affect the results, researchers conducted a blind re-labeling experiment. Three different annotators randomly re-annotated all 78 scenes of the "gold standard" tool combination in a blind manner. The consistency among the three annotators was measured by Fleiss' kappa coefficient, which resulted in 0.894 (generally considered as kappa > 0.8 indicating high consistency). The Cohen's kappa range between two annotators was [0.883, 0.908]. When re-evaluating the LLM controller on the re-labeled version, the score range was [0.875, 0.902], still significantly higher than the optimal router's [0.585, 0.601] on the re-labeled version. This indicates that the superior performance of the LLM controller is not due to labeling bias but rather its genuine strategy reasoning capability.
5.3 Closed-Loop Correct Action Rate Intention Recognition Benchmark
The evaluation focused only on the "intention matching" ability of the LLM—whether it outputs the correct tool? However, more importantly: during the multi-step closed-loop active learning loop, does each step of the LLM truly bring positive system performance improvements?
To this end, Agentic AMR designed a closed-loop correct action rate (Closed-Loop Correct-Action Rate) evaluation. Each test scenario included 5 random seeds with 40 independent trials (8 scenarios × 5 seeds), assessing whether the controller's model accuracy after sampling steps is on the right track compared to the optimal path of the oracle policy.
The results showed that the Qwen3-30B-A3B controller achieved a correct action rate of 0.375, while the Qwen3-8B reached 0.350, both significantly better than the ReAct baseline method (Mistral-7B's 0.250, Gemma-4-12B's 0.200) and fixed policy pipelines and learned routers (both at 0.250).
It is noteworthy that Qwen3-30B-A3B also demonstrates self-correction capabilities: in 37.5% of steps where a decision was deemed incorrect, the LLM controller could detect issues and correct them in subsequent steps (self-correction rate of 0.375), while smaller models (Qwen3-8B, Mistral-7B) had a zero self-correction rate. This indicates that larger models not only make more accurate decisions in one step but also exhibit stronger reflection and correction capabilities in long-term closed-loop control.

Why is this architecture secure? Wireless communication and electronic reconnaissance are highly sensitive to security. The architecture design of Agentic AMR ensures system security and auditability from multiple levels:
Firstly, the LLM never interacts with raw signal data. As a text model, it only reads numerical summaries and does not understand or process IQ signals. This means that even if the output of the LLM were incorrect, it would not directly interfere with the underlying signal processing process.
Secondly, all thresholded numerical operations maintain determinism. Sample selection, budget allocation for labeling, classification update—numerical operations that have a direct impact on system security and performance—are all performed by deterministic algorithms, without random sampling by the LLM. The LLM only handles "policy leaning" (i.e., preference weights for various criteria), and its execution logic is not influenced by the randomness of the LLM.
Thirdly, complete traceability of decisions. Every step of the system's decision-making is recorded: what RF state card the LLM read, what weight vector it outputted, which samples it ultimately selected, and how the model performance changed—this complete causal chain can be reviewed in detail after the fact, eliminating "black box decisions."
Fourthly, a safety fallback (Fallback). When the LLM fails to call or cannot correctly interpret its output, the system automatically falls back to a predefined deterministic rule controller to ensure that the system continues to function even during communication interruptions or when the LLM service is abnormal.
VII. Discussion: The paradigmatic significance of LLM as the "control plane"
The work of Agentic AMR not only addresses a specific engineering problem but also proposes an AI system design paradigm with broad applicability: LLM as the control plane.
In this paradigm, LLM is positioned as the "brain" or "commander" of the system, responsible for high-level situational perception and strategic planning without participating in low-level perception and operation. This division of labor is highly similar to the command chain structure in human organizations: Generals read intelligence summaries to make strategic decisions, while soldiers execute specific tasks on the front lines. Generals do not need to shoot directly, and soldiers do not need to possess strategic thinking.
The potential applications of this design paradigm are far beyond radio frequency signal processing. In autonomous driving, LLM can read the structured summaries of sensor data from cameras, radar, GPS, etc., plan global driving strategies (when to change lanes, when to slow down), but the underlying control signals (steering angles, throttle opening) are generated by deterministic controllers. In industrial automation, LLM can adjust production plans based on statistical summaries of sensor data from production lines, but the specific mechanical operations are still executed by PLC controllers.
The key is to find the right level of abstraction: LLM needs enough information to make decisions, but it should not be overwhelmed by too much raw data; LLM needs sufficient control over its strategy intelligence but should not directly interact with safety-critical operations.
VIII. Conclusion
Agentic AMR proposes a zero-sample combination tool planning framework for online adaptive modulation recognition. Its core contributions can be summarized in three points:
(1) IQ-Free control plane design. By constructing RF state cards and candidate pool cards as a unified abstract interface between LLM and the signal recognition layer, the high-level strategy reasoning and low-level signal processing are completely decoupled.
(2) Deterministic tool combination planning. LLM controllers perform weight planning in the semantic space of five major selection criteria, rather than directly selecting samples or outputting labels, ensuring the core numerical operations' determinacy and auditability.
(3) Comprehensive Non-stationary Environment Assessment. System evaluations conducted on five drift modes, multiple LLM back-ends, and various random seeds demonstrate that the LLM controller's tool planning capabilities under zero-shot scenarios (F1 score of 0.856-0.921) significantly outperform traditional training routers (F1 score of 0.567), exhibiting sustained decision accuracy and self-correction capabilities in closed-loop control.
This work suggests that integrating the semantic reasoning capability of large language models with domain-specific deterministic tools is a viable and reliable approach to introducing AI intelligence into safety-critical systems. When the LLM ceases to attempt to do everything itself and instead focuses on its most adept high-level strategy planning, AI systems can significantly enhance their security, interpretability, and generalization capabilities.
